Kepler's second law

A line joining the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.

A planet is not carried around its orbit at a constant speed. As it falls toward the Sun it quickens; as it climbs away it slows. The imaginary spoke between the two bodies covers a long, thin wedge when the planet is near, and a short, fat wedge when it is far — yet both wedges have exactly the same area for the same stretch of time. What our brass plate renders as a tidy ellipse is, in the sky, a continuous trade between distance and speed.

Real periods, compressed distances

The rhythm here is true; the ruler is not.

Each planet's angular speed is set by its measured sidereal period, so the tempo is faithful: Mercury races through four turns while Earth makes one, and Neptune hardly stirs in a lifetime. The radii, however, are compressed so all eight orbits fit on a single plate. Drawn to scale, Neptune's ring would be roughly thirty times Earth's and Mercury would vanish into the Sun's glare. Trust the tempo; forgive the distances.
